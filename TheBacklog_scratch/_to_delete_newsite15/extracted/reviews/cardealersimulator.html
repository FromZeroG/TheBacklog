    Car Dealer Simulator

    A car-flipping business sim with a satisfying restoration loop and an endgame that runs out of things to do.

    Platform PC Genre Business Simulation Released May 2025 Expected Playtime ~18 Hrs+
    Official art · via Steam
    86% Delivered

    Car Dealer Simulator puts you behind a run-down dealership lot, buying wrecked cars, running them through a restoration loop, and negotiating a price with buyers who have a hidden number in mind. The repair process breaks into real steps: pressure-washing a filthy exterior, a multi-stage rust-removal pass, swapping worn parts, and repainting before the car goes back on the lot. The negotiation system asks you to read a buyer's counter-offers and push back instead of accepting the first number, turning a sale into a small guessing game instead of a menu click.

    The shop grows around that loop. Hydraulic lifts and engine-swap stations cut down on repair time as you reinvest profit, and hiring staff eventually takes the most repetitive repair steps off your plate. Developer Garage Monkeys has kept updating the game since its May 2025 launch, and licensed radio stations playing over the shop floor add a small but real touch of atmosphere.

    The cracks show once the routine sets in. Individual repairs lean on repetitive mouse-drag animations that critics called tiresome by the tenth car, and the simulation underneath doesn't back up its own premise: a car rebuilt with top-quality parts drives identically to one patched together with worse ones, which undercuts the word "simulator" on the box. NPCs repeat the same handful of voice lines and wander into traffic, the map runs a tight two kilometers, and performance dips into stutter on some rigs (one reviewer logged a hard crash at the 37-minute mark). Reach full dealership ownership and the loop stalls into wait-timer busywork, a complaint that shows up independently across Steam user reviews. The May launch needed roughly sixteen hotfixes in its first two weeks, which players are split on: proof of a broken launch to some, proof the developer showed up to others.

    Steam rates it Very Positive, high 80s percent, though the exact review count varies by source enough that a false-precision number isn't worth printing here; the recent 30-day figure holds in the mid-80s too. Critic coverage stayed thin: GameGrin is the only outlet Metacritic tracked, landing at 65, while independent reviews elsewhere ranged from a skeptical pan to a solid 7 out of 10.

    Buy it, if the restoration loop is the draw. The business ceiling arrives faster than the price tag suggests, so don't expect the back half to hold your attention the way the first dealership does.
